# Empirical evidence: heel elevation and plantar/foot length

## What do human 3D foot-anthropometry studies report as heel height changes?

### Takeaway

Three primary studies directly compare measured foot dimensions across raised-heel conditions. Their conventional axial foot/arch length measures generally shorten with higher heels, by roughly 5 mm for medial arch length from flat to 80 mm elevation in one study and about 20–25 mm for projected full-foot length from low to high heel in two others. These results are relevant evidence that heel elevation changes measured foot geometry, but none establishes how a flexible tape’s path along the plantar skin from heel to toe changes.

### Cited Findings

- **Kouchi & Tsutsumi (2000), “3D Foot Shape and Shoe Heel Height,” Anthropological Science 108(4), 331–343, DOI [10.1537/ase.108.331](https://doi.org/10.1537/ase.108.331).** Original full article/PDF was accessible through [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ase1993/108/4/108_4_331/_article/-char/en) (PDF: [108_4_331](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ase1993/108/4/108_4_331/_pdf)). It measured 39 adult women without visible foot pathology, right foot, in static standing at 0, 40 and 80 mm simulated heel elevation; participants distributed weight equally over both feet (methods, pp. 332–334). It optically scanned the distal lower leg and dorsum, then made an alginate impression of the gap between plantar foot and platform and 3D-measured that plantar mold (p. 333). **Medial arch length** was defined as the distance MT-to-medial-heel-point (MH) parallel to the X-axis—not along the curved skin surface. Table 2 (p. 337) reports 135.4 ± 6.1 mm, 134.0 ± 6.3 mm and 130.0 ± 6.2 mm across 0, 40 and 80 mm: flat-to-80 mm change −5.4 mm (−4.0%, calculated from reported means). The authors report all differences among the three conditions significant at the 1% level for medial arch length. **Lateral arch length**, likewise a projected MT/MH-style linear measure (MF-to-lateral heel LH, parallel to X), was 114.8 ± 6.5, 115.0 ± 6.9 and 113.7 ± 6.9 mm; 0-to-80 mm change −1.1 mm (−1.0%). They state only condition 3 was shorter at p<.05; conditions 1 and 2 were not significantly different (pp. 336–337). The authors explicitly caution that heel-height/shape change was nonlinear and recommend more heel levels (p. 342). **Match:** closest source for a plantar underside, because it acquired a plantar impression; still not the target tape-path metric.
- **Wan, Yick & Yu (2017), “Validation of a 3D foot scanning system for evaluation of forefoot shape with elevated heels,” Measurement 99, 134–144, DOI [10.1016/j.measurement.2016.12.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.measurement.2016.12.005).** Full accepted manuscript is openly accessible in the [Hong Kong Polytechnic University repository](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/handle/10397/65812) ([PDF](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/65812/1/7_MEASUREMENT_%28PIRA%29.pdf)); it is marked pre-published version. Methods/abstract report 45 female subjects, right foot scanned standing in balanced posture at 0, 50 and 100 mm heel elevation, each foot supporting about half of body load; only forefoot dimensions were analyzed (pp. 1, 10–13). Their toe lengths are distances from a projected tread point to toe tips along the projected foot axis, not heel-to-toe or plantar surface paths. Table 4 (p. 16) gives hallux length 72.301 ± 4.51 mm (0 mm), 73.698 ± 4.37 (50 mm), 70.788 ± 4.54 (100 mm); second-toe length 66.753 ± 5.10, 68.473 ± 5.18, 65.492 ± 5.32 mm (both overall p<.001). At 100 mm, lateral forefoot joints rose: fourth MPJ height 22.418 ± 1.59 to 25.101 ± 1.94 mm; fifth MPJ 20.216 ± 2.05 to 25.450 ± 2.18 mm (0-to-100 mm, Table 4, p. 16). Crucially, the authors state: “the foot plantar is not scanned in this study” (limitations, p. 20). **Match:** direct measured geometry and forefoot/toe repositioning, but not plantar path length; its toe-length direction is also non-monotonic (peaks at 50 mm).
- **Zhu & Lee (2022), “Changes on Foot Dimensions With Elevated Heel Heights: A Pilot Study,” Digital Human Modeling and Applied Optimization 46, 63–69, DOI [10.54941/ahfe1001900](https://doi.org/10.54941/ahfe1001900).** The original full 7-page proceedings paper is [open access from AHFE](https://openaccess-api.cms-conferences.org/articles/download/978-1-958651-22-3_7). It enrolled 10 experienced high-heel-wearing healthy women (23 ± 3 years, shoe-foot sizes 235–240 mm) and scanned the right foot standing naturally, both feet on a footbed and weight balanced, at 30, 50, 70 and 90 mm heel heights; each scan was repeated twice and averaged (methods, pp. 64–65). It had **no flat/0-mm condition**. Foot length is defined as pternion-to-longest-toe distance along the foot axis (Table 1, p. 66), a straight projected anthropometric dimension. Table 2 (p. 66) reports foot length 230.615 mm at 30 mm vs 205.407 mm at 90 mm: −25.208 mm (−10.9%, calculated); ball-of-foot length 170.339 vs 146.485 mm (−23.854 mm); outside ball-of-foot length 144.964 vs 126.768 mm (−18.196 mm). Length effects are significant (p<.001; pp. 66–67). The paper calls the work preliminary and flags its small n and young female-only sample as limitations (p. 68). **Match:** supports heel-dependent shortening in straight axial foot dimensions, not a flat comparison or underside path.
- **Zhu & Lee (2023), “Differences in foot shape when wearing wedge-heeled shoes with elevated forefoot height and heel height,” Fashion and Textiles 10, Article 28, DOI [10.1186/s40691-023-00347-7](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40691-023-00347-7).** Springer’s article route redirected to an authorization page in this research session; the full open-access article was independently verified from the [German National Library’s archived publisher PDF](https://d-nb.info/1304595374/34), also listed by [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/713fb1854c4d4d80857bd6244ba0b27d). The study tested 35 experienced female wedge-shoe wearers, scanning the right foot standing naturally on the same shoe/footbed conditions; 10/20/30-mm forefoot elevations × 30/50/70/90-mm heel heights, no flat/0-mm control; scanner stated accuracy 1.0 mm (methods, pp. 3–5). It measured 14 dimensions twice and averaged them. “Foot length” is the Pte-to-longest-toe distance along the foot axis (Table 1, p. 8), not a skin-surface route. Table 3 (p. 10) shows the heel-height marginal means: 230.42 ± 6.07 mm at 30 mm vs 210.45 ± 7.74 mm at 90 mm, −19.97 mm (−8.7%, calculated); ball-of-foot length 169.51 ± 5.03 vs 150.70 ± 8.90 mm, −18.81 mm. Heel-height effects on foot length and ball length were significant (two-way ANOVA, p<.001, Table 2, p. 9). This compares raised heel footbeds, with variable forefoot elevation, not a flat-to-raised shoe. The authors also report a significant forefoot-height × heel-height interaction for foot length, and the 10 × 90 mm combination had the shortest foot length (204.44 ± 6.00 mm; pp. 10–12). **Match:** strong evidence of heel-dependent projected anthropometric dimensions in a static shoe-last design setting, but not the target plantar route.

### Inferences

- Under these study conditions, the measured foot/arch shape changes rather than acting as a rigid, invariant length: 3D anthropometry reports higher instep/navicular dimensions and shorter projected foot or arch dimensions, with region-specific effects (especially the lateral forefoot in Wan et al.). This supports treating heel elevation as a meaningful geometric condition, but does not specify the direction of a tape laid against the underside.
- Kouchi & Tsutsumi’s plantar impression is the most direct empirical access to underside geometry among the retrieved sources. Its reported arch length is nevertheless a straight distance projected on a coordinate axis; the authors do not report a geodesic/skin-following heel-to-toe trace or total plantar arc length.
- Axial heel-to-toe and ball-length changes in the 2022/2023 Zhu studies cannot be equated to an underside flexible-tape reading: a projected endpoint distance and a surface-following curve measure different quantities. Their shortening does not contradict an individual’s longer measured plantar path in a raised-heel posture.

### Gaps

- No located primary study directly reports paired flat-versus-elevated **heel-to-toe plantar skin surface-path length** (same anatomical endpoints, tape or computed plantar geodesic), with load and toe/MTP posture controlled/reported. In particular, no study above reports that exact tape quantity or a general correction factor for it.
- Most high-heel scan evidence is static and uses female participants, but only Kouchi & Tsutsumi includes a flat condition alongside 40/80 mm; that source gives arch lengths, not total heel-to-toe plantar path. The 2023 wedge study deliberately lacks a flat condition, and the 2022 pilot starts at 30 mm.
- The accessible sources report sample means/SDs and significance, not confidence intervals for paired path-length changes; there is no valid way to infer the uncertainty or population distribution of the user’s tape-path observation from these different endpoints.

## How do these studies define length, plantar surface, toe position and loading?

### Takeaway

The sources distinguish several easily conflated quantities: straight/projected overall foot length; projected heel-to-ball/arch dimensions; toe segment lengths referenced to the forefoot; plantar pressure; and plantar-fascia strain. Only the first three are anthropometric length dimensions here, and none equals the requested heel-to-toe tape path along plantar skin.

### Cited Findings

- Kouchi & Tsutsumi’s 3D protocol measured the foot’s dorsum optically and then recorded an alginate mold of the plantar gap (methods, p. 333); it defined medial arch length as MT-to-MH “measured parallel to the X-axis” and lateral arch length as MF-to-LH parallel to X (p. 336). The coordinate plane itself follows the elevated foot and is horizontal only in the flat condition (p. 334). Thus these published arch lengths are axis-parallel dimensions, not a tape following the plantar skin’s contour.
- Wan et al. explicitly defined the foot axis by pternion-to-second-MPJ projected onto the horizontal plane, with toe length from tread point to toe tip along that axis; their MPJ/DIP heights are measured from the platform normal to its surface (Table 2, pp. 12–13). This is projected toe/forefoot geometry, not plantar-path measurement. Their limitations say plantar surface was not scanned (p. 20).
- Zhu & Lee (2023) aligned digital models to the footbed plane and defined foot length as the distance along the foot axis from pternion to longest toe; ball lengths are likewise endpoint distances parallel to that axis (methods, pp. 6–8, Table 1, p. 8). Participants stood naturally without support on the footbeds; the opposite foot was placed on the same shoe to distribute weight (p. 5). These dimensions reflect static loaded foot shape under the specified footwear support, not walking strain or unweighted tissue length.
- Zhu & Lee (2022) likewise defined overall and ball foot lengths parallel to the projected foot axis, and scanned standing with both feet on the footbed in balanced loading (methods, pp. 3–4; Table 1, p. 66). Its 3D scanner was stated to have 1.0-mm accuracy (p. 65); the wedge study also states 1.0 mm scanner accuracy (p. 4). These are instrument specifications, not confidence limits for the reported biological change.
- **Do not substitute plantar fascia strain for plantar skin/path length.** For example, Wang et al. (2021) reports simulated plantar-fascia strain under 3/5/7-cm heels during walking in one subject, using musculoskeletal and finite-element models—not a measured skin path: [open full text in PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8720874/), especially Abstract and Methods §§2.1–2.3. That biomechanics endpoint cannot answer a tape-measure question.

### Inferences

- Heel elevation simultaneously changes ankle/foot posture, contact/support, forefoot/MTP geometry and loaded foot shape. The source measurements are conditioned on different support surfaces, heel definitions, toe posture, alignment references and loading; they are not interchangeable observations of “foot length.”
- The 2023 wedge study describes a 15° toe spring and pointed toe box on its standardized footbeds (methods, p. 4); the 2017 and 2000 studies use simulated support platforms. Consequently, one should not assume all studied toe/MTP positions or the plantar support line reproduce a person’s bare-foot test or a particular last.

### Gaps

- No reviewed scan study simultaneously captures full plantar skin, names common heel/toe endpoints, computes the plantar-surface route between them, and compares that route with a tape under matched flat and heel-raised loading.
- These studies do not isolate whether altered plantar length comes from skin stretch, skeletal/joint posture, tissue compression, footbed support, toe extension/flexion, or combinations. The available measured axial length outcomes are not a tissue-mechanics mechanism test.

## Does published empirical evidence support the user’s >1 cm plantar tape difference or a general correction formula?

### Takeaway

There is published empirical support for heel-dependent 3D foot shape and for substantial changes in projected anthropometric length measures; the closest flat-to-raised medial-arch result is only −5.4 mm in a sample mean and is a projected length, not the user’s plantar tape path. I found no study measuring that exact quantity, so this evidence neither validates nor refutes the individual >1 cm tape observation and supplies no defensible generic correction formula.

### Cited Findings

- The strongest flat-vs-raised full-foot-shape study, Kouchi & Tsutsumi (n=39; 0/40/80 mm), reports medial arch shortening from 135.4 to 130.0 mm (−5.4 mm) and lateral arch change from 114.8 to 113.7 mm (−1.1 mm) (Table 2, p. 337). Their paper says the relation between heel height and foot-shape changes “was not linear” and further study was necessary (p. 342). These are sample mean axial arch dimensions, not subject-level plantar tape paths.
- Two separate elevated-heel 3D anthropometry samples show larger shortening over 30-to-90-mm heel heights: Zhu & Lee 2022 n=10: foot length −25.208 mm (Table 2, p. 66); Zhu & Lee 2023 n=35: 30-to-90-mm heel marginal means −19.97 mm (Table 3, p. 10). Both start at a raised heel, define foot length as a straight foot-axis dimension and do not measure plantar surface path.
- The 2017 Wan study includes 0/50/100 mm and demonstrates altered forefoot/toe measures, but explicitly did not scan plantar surface (methods/table 4, pp. 12–18; limitation, p. 20). It therefore cannot serve as a hidden direct observation of underside length.

### Inferences

- **Direct match:** none of the retrieved empirical studies directly measures the user’s specified underside heel-to-toe tape route. **Closest match:** Kouchi & Tsutsumi measured a plantar mold and arch geometry, but quantified projected arch distances; **indirect support:** the 3D scan studies establish posture-dependent shortening/shape change in conventional foot-length metrics.
- The varied sizes/signs across different endpoints and conditions, together with Kouchi & Tsutsumi’s nonlinearity warning and Zhu & Lee 2023’s forefoot-height interaction, argue against treating any single published average as a universal scalar adjustment. This is a boundary on what the evidence supports, not an implementation recommendation.
- The user’s reported personal tape result remains a distinct uncontrolled observation. Published projected-length shortening does not establish that a skin-following tape path must shorten; the measurands are not equivalent.

### Gaps

- Needed primary experiment for the exact question: repeated paired scans or surface-path measurement on the same feet in flat and defined heel/footbed conditions; mark reproducible plantar endpoints; fix and report weight-bearing and toe/MTP posture; compute the geodesic/path length and compare it with repeatable tape measurements; report participant-level deltas and uncertainty. No such study was found in this bounded search.
- The search found open full-text primary sources from 2000, 2017, 2022 and 2023; that is useful evidence, not an exhaustive systematic review, and should not be interpreted as proof that no direct study exists anywhere.
