# Posture, measurement uncertainty and fit: evidence relay

2026-10-27

These compact notes preserve the third independent researcher's supplied findings. That researcher read the full texts identified below; this synthesis writer has not independently reopened them. This is an evidence relay, not a new literature review. Proposals and geometric deductions are labelled separately.

## Loading and repeatability

**McPoil et al. (2008), [Arch height change during sit-to-stand](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2553777/). Access: firsthand full-text methods/results, relayed.** Study of 275 healthy participants, mean age 26.3, comparing sitting with relaxed standing at approximately 50% load per foot. Mean dorsal arch height was approximately 10 mm lower standing. Inter-rater SEM was 0.7 mm for weightbearing arch height and 1.5 mm for weightbearing foot length. Repeated readings were from stored images: these are not participant-repositioning/day-retest errors and not tape-girth tolerances. Useful conclusion: retain loading conditions; do not import those numbers as project tolerances.

**Allan et al. (2023), [scoping review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10134589/). Access: firsthand full text, relayed.** Across 78 studies, loading definitions, terminology, markers and reporting were inconsistent. Seated protocols described as non-weightbearing could still involve mechanical foot contact. One-/two-dimensional observations omit surface information. Do not equate seated with unloaded or treat a posture label as a complete loading protocol.

## Elevated-foot conditions are not a universal correction

**Zhu and Lee (2023), [Differences in foot shape when wearing wedge-heeled shoes](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40691-023-00347-7). Access: firsthand full-text methods/results, relayed.** Thirty-five healthy experienced young women, mean age 22.1; dominant right foot; 12 wedge conditions combining forefoot elevations of 10/20/30 mm with heel elevations of 30/50/70/90 mm. Participants stood naturally without assistance, opposite foot on a matched shoe. Two scans per condition, late morning, with five-minute rests.

Higher heel elevation was associated with smaller foot/ball lengths and widths and larger ball/instep girths and instep/navicular heights; forefoot elevation moderated effects. These population, support and posture conditions do not establish a universal girth change or heel-to-last correction. Record heel and forefoot elevation/drop separately and observe the intended supported posture rather than transforming a baseline by formula.

## Methods and fit allowances measure different things

**Lee, Lin and Wang (2014), [Comparing 3D foot scanning with conventional measurement methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4215017/). Access: firsthand full-text methods/results, relayed.** Participants: 130 healthy Taiwanese university students, 65 women/65 men, ages 18–30. Trained operator; two repetitions per method; relaxed balanced bipedal weightbearing, late morning. Measurement method significantly affected dimensions; tracing cannot capture height, and footprint/contact and soft-tissue outline are not interchangeable. Reported mean absolute differences across linear dimensions were 0.73–1.50 mm for scanner, 0.80–2.40 mm for calipers and 1.20–2.65 mm for ink print. These are not tape-girth errors or a justified project-wide ±mm threshold.

**ISO 19409:2022, [official catalogue/public abstract](https://www.iso.org/standard/81028.html). Access: firsthand public abstract/preview only, relayed; full standard unread.** Scope concerns physical or virtual last dimensions, which do not necessarily correspond to anatomical foot positions/dimensions. It is not evidence for automatic allowances or a standard foot-capture protocol; no inaccessible clause or station rule is claimed.

**Buldt and Menz (2018), [footwear fit review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6064070/). Access: firsthand full text, relayed.** Eighteen studies; fit definitions were inconsistent and included multiple dimensions, activity and qualitative judgements. Their 10–20 mm shoe toe-clearance recommendation is not a universal last allowance. Construction-specific physical fit trials remain necessary. The selected evidence does not quantify separate corrections for upper, lining, stiffener, socks, closure, style or activity.

## Proposed capture and uncertainty records—not a normative protocol

Choose one named practitioner protocol. Record each foot, load/posture, socks/orthotics, time, operator, tension instruction and actual photographed/sketched tape route, including dorsal/plantar landmarks. Capture upright-pencil outlines with declared axis/landmarks, lengths, widths at declared stations, ball/instep and selected mid/waist loop; add short/long heel where relevant. A narrow underfoot trace, outside projected outline and contact print must be distinguished. Optional ruler heights and side photographs are sparse observations, not complete sections.

Three repeats, median and range are proposed practical quality control, not a literature-wide rule. For high heels, retain both baseline and intended supported heel/forefoot pose; stable supports/helper are proposed arrangements, not clinically validated loading distributions. Keep repeat spread, natural posture/load variation, authored geometry/smoothing and fit allowances separate.

Geometric deduction: circumference plus width cannot uniquely determine dorsal/lower curvature or asymmetry. Without a gauge or scan, those profile features remain authored assumptions. No formula, numeric allowance, automatic section reconstruction or 3D implementation follows from these sources.
