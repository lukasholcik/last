# Last sections and measurement evidence

2026-10-27

Anatomical landmarks and shoemaking measurements can guide editable last sections, but they do not supply a complete section system. A tape girth is not automatically a planar cross-section; heel wraps especially need their actual surface paths preserved. Without a contour gauge or scan, most profiles remain authored geometry constrained by observations—not reconstructed anatomy. The proposal below supports measurement and sparse profile review only. Full 3D implementation awaits Lukas’s approval.

## Measurement names do not determine section planes

Practitioner instructions differ even when labels match. The table preserves those differences rather than inventing a common anatomical plane. Sources [1–5] document foot measurements, not mandatory last-section schedules.

| Region | Verified path or limitation | Useful constraint, not an automatic ring |
|---|---|---|
| Ball/joint | 3DShoemaker: shortest tape route through the sides of the first/fifth ball joints. Wayman: widest part, big-toe joint to little-toe-side bulge; mark tape angle. Neither specifies a plane. [1,3] | Preserve MTP1/MTP5 separately, including longitudinal offset. Two points cannot uniquely determine a plane: any proposed section requires a declared third constraint. |
| Waist/mid | 3DShoemaker uses averaged-plane/midpoint wording between ball and instep; this does not uniquely specify a 3D manual tape route. Wayman places B exactly between A and C; equivalent paths are not established. [1,3] | A protocol-defined mid measurement; not automatically the narrowest arch or highest instep. |
| Instep | 3DShoemaker uses the middle-cuneiform prominence and shortest plantar loop. Wayman uses a narrowest mid-arch route to about 2 cm above its subjectively identified instep bump. Anatomical equivalence is unverified. [1,3] | Record the actual dorsal point and plantar route; neither instruction establishes a unique section plane. |
| Heel/rearfoot | Wayman requests widest heel width without a station; 3DShoemaker measures tracing width perpendicular to its heel axis at one-sixth foot length. [2,3] | Choose and label a width station; neither width supplies a heel profile or girth. |
| Short heel | Amara Hark Weber’s reported fitting wraps behind the heel toward the foot–leg junction, without precise palpable/plantar landmarks or plane. [4] | A separately documented diagonal surface loop, not an ordinary transverse section. |
| Long heel / long instep | Wayman D runs from just above its instep-bump centre around the ground-contact heel and back. Weber’s long heel lies nearer the instep than her short heel. No plane is fixed. [3,4] | Retain each named path. “Long instep” equivalence is unverified; IEEE’s compiled “instep-to-heel” name does not establish it. [5] |
| Toe | No universal toe-height station or toe-box girth was verified. | Observe toe envelope/extent; author toe shape, height and endpoint as style controls. |
| Topline/topcut | No universal station or angle was verified. | Distinguish the last’s upper termination from the shoe upper’s style-dependent opening. |

IEEE Table 26 (§3.3.20, PDF pp. 47–48) calls long heel “instep-to-heel girth”: variants use landing point and instep at 50% or 55% foot length, with differing provider axes. TechMed methods 3/4 specify 35° from horizontal for long heel only, without signed tilt direction. Table 27 (§3.3.21, pp. 49–50) gives short-heel routes through the landing point, including a shortest loop or a route through a tentative junction defined by ankle crease/fifth-toe extensor tendon (§3.2.4, p. 14). [5] These examples do not uniquely fix our 3D plane from two landmarks or axis-perpendicular wording; our orientation remains unresolved, not automatically orthogonal.

IEEE compiles conflicting heel definitions, including plane-based definitions; it is explicitly not an IEEE Standard and excludes last measurements. Those definitions cannot make manual contact loops planar. [5] The 2013 *Shoe-last design templates* chapter was accessible only as metadata/abstract: its full text and figures were unavailable. Its verified authors are Yan Luximon and A. Luximon, pp. 216–235, DOI 10.1533/9780857098795.3.216. The 2021 chapter title/number was index-only; first-edition metadata must not be transferred to it. No detailed section rule here is attributed to either edition. [11]

## Sparse controls must expose authored assumptions

Proposed minimum: rear-heel extent/profile plus one declared heel-width section; one selected midfoot section, adding a separate waist or instep section only when distinct observations justify it; a ball-region section tied to independent medial/lateral landmarks; a fore-toe shape/height section and toe endpoint; and a style-selected topcut boundary. Topcut need not be a ring. Short/long heel remain separate surface-path diagnostics. This is a candidate control set, not six universal sections.

For every actual section, declare its datum, location, orientation and evidence source. World-vertical, local-centreline-normal and landmark-oblique frames are different choices. Width, height, medial/lateral upper shape and lower curvature must remain explicitly authored wherever unobserved; compute perimeter for comparison, not as a shape generator.

The geometric reason is simple: circumference plus width does not uniquely determine a profile, dorsum, arch or asymmetry. Paper outlines and tape supply useful lengths, widths and loop totals; optional ruler heights/side photographs add sparse observations, not complete sections. A projected outside outline, plantar contact print and designed last feather edge—the boundary where upper meets bottom—are different curves; feather-edge geometry also matters when considering allowances. [12] Conventional measurement methods produce different results; tracing cannot capture height. [8] ISO 19409 concerns physical/virtual last dimensions, which need not coincide with anatomical foot dimensions; its public abstract is not a foot-to-last conversion rule. [10]

## Capture conditions and keep uncertainty separate from fit

Proposed protocol: choose one named practitioner method, document any departure, and record each foot separately. Capture paper outlines with an upright pencil, a declared heel/forefoot axis and landmarks; distinguish the outside boundary from a narrow underfoot trace or contact print. Record length, widths at declared stations, ball, selected instep and protocol-defined mid/waist loop; add short/long heel where relevant. Photograph or sketch every tape route, including plantar contact. Store load/posture, socks/orthotics, tape tension instruction, time and operator. Three repeats with median and range are proposed practical quality control, not a published universal standard.

Do not blend incompatible protocols: 3DShoemaker specifies seated semi-weightbearing barefoot measurement; Wayman specifies standing in intended socks, afternoon, with fit-preference tension; Weber’s reported fitting uses seated right-angle posture before noon and intended socks. [1,3,4] A scoping review also found inconsistent loading definitions, so “seated” must not silently mean unloaded. [6]

For high heels, capture a baseline and a separate intended, supported pose. Record heel rise and forefoot elevation/drop separately; a helper and stable support are proposed practical arrangements, not validated loading distributions. Research on 35 young experienced female wearers on tested wedges found condition-specific changes in lengths, widths, girths and heights, moderated by forefoot elevation. It does not justify a universal heel-height correction. [7]

Keep repeat spread, posture/load variation, authored geometry/smoothing and fit allowances as separate records. Method-comparison errors are not tape-girth tolerances. [8] Fit definitions are inconsistent and multidimensional; published shoe-clearance advice is not a universal last allowance. [9] Record construction, upper/lining/stiffener, socks, closure, style and activity as proposed design context—not quantified corrections. Ballet-flat retention and high-heel requirements require explicit fit intent and construction-specific physical trials, not literature-disguised defaults.
